# Customer Discovery Outreach Playbook

**Purpose:** Week-one outreach targeting 20 messages → 3–5 conversations → first interviews logged in `research_log.md` section 6.

> **Updated 2026-10-12.** The approach changed on 2026-10-11: lead with a **free report** on one client the contractor is unsure about, then ask for 15 minutes of feedback. The gift card is **$50** (Home Depot or Lowe's). The scripts below were written before the free-report approach; the free-report message is drafted separately. Record every interviewee's **trade**. There's no trade-first choice, but watch for clusters.

---

## Geography

**Discovery is national.** The pain (commercial customer payment risk) is not Florida-specific; talking to a frustrated HVAC owner in Phoenix or Atlanta is as useful for validation as one in Jacksonville. Cold LinkedIn outreach opens up to any US metro with a lot of small commercial trade activity. Current target metros: **Jacksonville, Tampa, Atlanta, Charlotte, Phoenix**.

**Phone calls and supply house visits stay Jacksonville-local** because they have to — you can't fly to Phoenix to drop donuts at Ferguson.

| Channel | Count | Geography | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm network (text/call people you know) | 5–8 | Any | Highest conversion. People who know you say yes. |
| LinkedIn cold (DBPR + web-sourced names) | 6–8 | National | Slower, but builds a repeatable pipeline. |

| Supply house counter (Ferguson, Johnstone, Winsupply) | 2 visits | Jacksonville | Not messages — face-to-face referrals from people who see 50+ contractors/week. |

Goal: 3–5 yeses. Don't optimize for sends; optimize for "yes, let's talk."

## LinkedIn — Cold (no mutual connection)

> Hi [Name] — I'm working on a tool to help small trade contractors check out new commercial customers *before* taking the job — court records, liens, payment history, that kind of thing. I spent 5 years inside Amex on SMB credit, so I have a sense of what signals matter, but I need to learn how contractors actually think about this.
>
> Could I get 15 minutes by phone in the next week or two? Just learning, not selling. $50 Home Depot or Lowe's card for your time.
>
> If now's a bad time, no worries.

**Why the structure:** Work first ("I'm working on a tool…"), Amex second (supporting context, not headline). "I need to learn how contractors actually think about this" frames *them* as the expert, which is true and disarming. Easy out at the end lowers pressure to say yes — counterintuitively increases reply rate.

**Note:** Removed location-specific opener since outreach is now national. Add location only when it's a credibility signal (e.g., reaching a Florida contractor: "I'm in Jacksonville…").

## Personal network — text or call

No script. Just sound like yourself.

> "Hey, working on a side project — tool to help small contractors check out new commercial customers before taking a job. You know anyone running an HVAC/plumbing/electrical shop who'd talk to me 15 minutes? I'll buy lunch."

For people you know who *are* contractors:

> "Hey, can I ask you 15 minutes of questions about how you decide which commercial jobs to take? Working on something."

## Phone — cold call (from DBPR phone numbers, Jacksonville only)

**Best call windows:** 10 AM – 2 PM weekdays. Avoid 7–9 AM (loading trucks), 4:30–6 PM (heading home), Mondays (catching up), Friday afternoons (gone).

**Script:**

> "Hi, is this [Mike]? Mike, my name's Andres — I'm calling from Jacksonville. I know it's a cold call so I'll be quick. I'm not selling anything. I'm an engineer building a tool to help contractors check out new commercial customers before taking the job, and I'm trying to learn from people actually running shops. I spent 5 years at Amex on the credit side. Could I get 15 minutes of your time sometime this week or next? I'll send a $50 Home Depot or Lowe's card."

Then **shut up.** Resist filling silence. Let them respond.

- If yes → "Great — what works better for you, tomorrow morning or Thursday afternoon?" Lock the time.
- If "not now" → "When's a better time to try back?"
- If hard no → "Thanks for your time. Have a good one." Hang up. Move on.

**Don't:** explain more, sweeten the offer, defend yourself. The 30-second pitch above is the whole pitch.

## Supply house — in-person (Jacksonville only)

Counter staff at Ferguson, Johnstone Supply, Winsupply talk to 50+ contractors a week. They know who's reasonable, who's been around forever, who'll actually have a conversation.

**How:**
1. Pick one branch. Show up mid-morning (after the 7–9 AM rush, before lunch).
2. Bring a box of donuts and coffee for the counter staff. Not optional.
3. Talk to the counter manager, not the cashier.

**What to say:**

> "Hey — I'm working on a tool for small trade contractors around customer credit checks. Trying to learn from owners, not sell anything. Any of your regulars who'd be willing to talk to me for 15 minutes? I'd send a $50 Home Depot or Lowe's card for their time."

You'll get 2–4 names per visit. In your follow-up message:

> "[Counter person] at Ferguson said you'd be a good person to talk to about…"

That intro line will roughly triple your reply rate vs. pure cold.

## The Interview Script

15–20 minutes. Record only with permission. Take notes during, write the `research_log.md` entry within 30 minutes of hanging up.

### Opening (1 min)

> "Thanks for the time. I'm researching how contractors decide whether to take on new commercial customers — specifically around payment risk. I'm not selling anything, just trying to understand your world. No right or wrong answers. Mind if I take notes?"

### Story first (5 min) — *most important section*

> "Tell me about the last time a commercial customer didn't pay you, or paid really late. Walk me through what happened."

Then **shut up.** Let them talk. Prompts only:
- "What happened next?"
- "How did that feel?"
- "How did you handle it?"

Don't lead. Don't sympathize too much. Don't pitch.

### Current process (5 min)

- "When a new commercial customer calls you, walk me through what happens between the first call and you starting the job."
- "How do you decide whether to extend net-30?"
- "Do you ever ask for a deposit? When?"
- "Have you ever walked away from a job because you didn't trust they'd pay?"
- "What do you do today to check someone out? Google? Their website? Anything else?"

### Probe deeper (3 min)

- "Have you ever pulled a business credit report — D&B, Experian Business, anything like that?" (Most likely no — that itself is data.)
- "Have you ever heard of Levelset?" (If yes: "What do you use it for? What works? What doesn't?")
- "If you had to put a dollar number on bad debt in a typical year, what would it be?"

### Negative validation (1 min) — *don't skip this*

> "Has there been a year where payment risk wasn't really an issue? What was different?"

If the pain is real, they'll push back on the premise. If they shrug and say "honestly it's not really a problem," that's the most valuable data you'll get all day. Take it seriously.

### Solution test (3 min) — *only after everything above*

- "Imagine a tool where you type in a customer name and address and get back a 1-page risk report in 30 seconds — court records, liens, business history. What would that be worth to you per month?"
- "What would have to be true for you to actually use it?"
- "What's missing from that idea?"

### Close (1 min)

- "If I build a prototype in the next few months, would you be willing to look at it?"
- "Who else should I be talking to?" — **ask for referrals every single time.**

Send the gift card the same day. Reliability builds reputation faster than anything you'll say.

## Rules That Matter More Than the Script

1. **Never pitch.** The moment you start selling, they start lying to be polite. Useless data.
2. **Don't lead the witness.** "Wouldn't it be great if you could check customers instantly?" is poison. "What do you do today when a new customer calls?" is gold.
3. **Trust actions, not words.** "I'd pay for that" in an interview is worth nothing. Ask if they've ever paid for *anything* like it before.
4. **Write the summary within the hour.** Memory degrades fast. Quotes you don't capture in 60 minutes are gone.
5. **Look for emotion and specifics.** "Yeah, we get burned sometimes" = useless. "Three years ago a property manager stiffed me for $42K and I had to skip payroll" = gold. Emotional intensity tells you the size of the pain.

After 5 interviews you'll have one of three outcomes:
- **Clear validation:** people describe the pain unprompted, in specific dollar figures, and "yes, I'd pay for that" comes from multiple people who've paid for adjacent tools.
- **Clear invalidation:** people shrug, name workarounds that already work, or describe a different problem as bigger.
- **Pivot signal:** a different problem keeps surfacing in every conversation. That's the most valuable outcome — you've discovered the real wedge.

All three are wins compared to spending three months building the wrong thing.
